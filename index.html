A small Texas city that chose to govern itself — and kept the stories to prove it.

Incorporated July 3, 1951  —  2026

The Story

Castle Hills is an independent city ringed by San Antonio, Texas. It was incorporated on July 3, 1951, after its residents — who had been meeting since 1950 as the Castle Hills Improvement League — decided they would rather provide their own fire protection, police, water, and streets than be annexed by the growing city next door.

This page is an online companion to the Castle Hills Historical Society's 75th-anniversary exhibit. Everything here — the dates, the names, the quotes, the captions — comes from the exhibit's own panels and from the newspaper clippings displayed alongside them. It is a portrait of a community told largely in the words it chose for itself.

“There are no groundbreaking ceremonies planned. Castle Hills can't afford a silver spade.” — Chester Slimp, acting secretary of Castle Hills, on beginning construction of the first City Hall, 1955

The Spine of the Story

A Timeline

Dated milestones from the exhibit boards and the clippings shown with them — woven together with a few independently verified records (marked ✓ verified).

1856

Before the city, the surveyors

Handwritten field notes survey 165½ acres of this land — Survey No. 367, made for James G. Brien. Maps and family parcels (Prinz, Slimp, and others) would shape the streets to come.

1921 – 1923

The Castle on the hill

Chester and Helen Slimp buy 35.5 acres on Lockhill-Selma Road for $3,500. Their rock house, designed by architect Ralph Cameron, becomes the landmark that gives Castle Hills its name.

1951

A city is born

Residents vote to incorporate. The City of Castle Hills becomes official in 1951 — a Texas General Law Type A city, governed by a mayor and five aldermen, with Leonard Yeckel as its first mayor. ✓ verified: TML / TSHA.

1956

The casino on West Avenue

Ten officers raid a “swank” bookmaking house at 6701 West Avenue, owned by Tom Moore — the home whose high-stakes poker games would echo all the way to Reno.

1961 – 1962

The Dibble case

A prominent resident shoots his wife, Sabina Dibble, at their home on Dogwood. A jury finds him insane and commits him to Rusk State Hospital.

1970

The city reaches its peak

The U.S. Census counts 5,311 residents — Castle Hills' high-water mark. Built out and ringed by San Antonio with nowhere to expand, the population has eased gently downward in every census since. ✓ verified: U.S. Census / Texas Almanac.

1972

The bombing of King Arthur's Court

A pre-dawn blast tears through the King Arthur's Court night club on Jackson-Keller Road, waking the North Side. The FBI investigates a possible extortion plot.

1974

Four at once — and an unsolved party

The Hansen Quadruplets — Allison, Brooke, Claire, and Darcy — are born and become San Antonio's babies. The same season, an aircraft executive is bludgeoned to death at a New Year's Eve party; the case is never solved.

The poker legacy

From West Avenue to the World Series

Tom Moore — later called a “Founding Father” of the World Series of Poker — left Castle Hills for Reno. Decades on, 85-year-old resident Gerald Silber cashed in a record-field WSOP Seniors event.

2003

The Commons of Castle Hills

The council sets aside the Lemonwood land between City Hall and the fire station as a public common. The Commons is dedicated on July 4, 2003 — the city's first Fourth of July celebration. ✓ verified: city records.

2026

Seventy-five years of community

Castle Hills marks its diamond anniversary — “1951–2026, 75 Years of Community” — with the exhibit this page is drawn from.

Chapter One

The Castle on the Hill

A family, a rock house, and the artist who chose the spot for its bluebonnets.

Chester & Helen Slimp

“Bluebonnets for blueprints, that's how I got this house.”

In 1921, Mr. & Mrs. Chester Slimp purchased 35.5 acres of land on Lockhill-Selma Road for $3,500. Their home, said the exhibit, “sits on a hill, and at the time it was built there was nothing around the home but some trees and brush. It was a landmark in the area.”

The architect was Ralph Cameron — known in Texas for the Medical Arts Building (now The Emily Morgan Hotel), the main Post Office near the Alamo, and Grace Lutheran Church. The builder was Charlie Bryant. The same house, the family noted, can be found in Terrell Hills.

Chester A. Slimp Sr. was born in Chattanooga, Tennessee in 1889, an oil distributor who was deeply involved in the YMCA and Baptist Temple Sunday School. He helped extend West Avenue to Lockhill-Selma in 1929 — the reason West Avenue still has its “dog leg” turn — and lived in his Castle until he died, age 75, in 1965.

per the city's accountThat the city took its name from this hilltop “Castle” is the Historical Society's and the city's own account; independent research found no deed or directory to corroborate it, so it is preserved here as local tradition.

Helen Ferne Goolsby Slimp

The artist who came to Texas to meet a cowboy

Born in Virginia in 1890, Helen came to Texas at nineteen “hoping to meet a cowboy.” She wasn't impressed with the cowboys — but she met Chester Slimp, and married him in 1913. She would live to be 105.

She worked as a stained-glass designer, with commissions for the Hermann Sons Lodge and area churches, and never stopped making art — glass, lithography, painting, pastels. It was her art, the exhibit says, that drew the family to the country on Lockhill-Selma, where she could paint while her children played.

She was a friend of Marion Koogler McNay and of Mount Rushmore sculptor Gutzon Borglum, and she taught art — her most famous student was the painter Porfirio Salinas.

Chapter Two

Birth of a City

In the first mayor's own words — and with no tax dollars to spend.

Leonard Yeckel, First Mayor

“We should take steps to incorporate our area”

In 1950 the residents formed the Castle Hills Improvement League, meeting in the auditorium of the new Castle Hills Elementary School to talk over fire and police protection, water, and garbage — even as San Antonio was annexing large areas “without the facilities to furnish these services.”

They hired attorney Ralph Brite, used existing streets and the railroad track as boundaries, and won state approval. When San Antonio declared the incorporation void “by some technicalities” and moved to annex the new city, Brite fought back — and “the City of Castle Hills became a reality” on July 3, 1951. Independent records confirm the 1951 incorporation and the city's General Law Type A form — a mayor and five aldermen ✓ verified — though no public record beyond the city's own account fixes the exact day. day: city's account

In the first year there were no tax funds at all. Yeckel later recalled that $20 was solicited from each house, a volunteer fire department served the city, and “most households contributed.”

Why a city at all?

Six reasons, one independent streak

The Historical Society lists the most likely reasons the founders chose self-government rather than absorption into San Antonio:

• Preserve country, small-town community living
• Provide for police protection
• Provide for fire protection
• Control post-WWII land development
• Provide for streets and drainage maintenance
• Fight annexation by San Antonio

Or, as the city's own framing puts it: a place “created by independent thinking, hardworking men and women who saw a need to come together and make Castle Hills a premier community.”

per the city's accountResisting annexation is the widely-held explanation, and Yeckel's own account supports it — but independent research turned up no primary or scholarly source that fixes the legal motive, so it stands as the community's well-supported tradition rather than a documented fact.

Chapter Three

Business & Nightlife

“General stores and western wear gave way to groceries and night clubs.”

A changing main street

From the country store to the supermarket

“As residents moved to Castle Hills, businesses followed,” the exhibit explains. “As our city became more cosmopolitan, new stores replaced the old ones. General stores and western wear gave way to groceries and night clubs, who in turn were replaced by supermarkets and sophisticated restaurants.”

The boards catalog them block by block along NW Military Highway and West Avenue — Piggly Wiggly and Winn's, Cloth World and Harry's Western Wear, Country Junction, the Royal Pharmacy, and a long line of restaurants from La Scala to Double Dave's Pizzaworks.

After dark

Dancing on the terrace

At the corner of West Avenue and Jackson-Keller Road, the Castle Hills Town Club offered the Ed Massey Combo “for your dancing pleasure on the terrace” — mambos, rhumbas, tangos, fox trots, and waltzes — plus the popular Sadie Hawkins dances. In 1957 the club redecorated with “an aquarium for tropical fish and fish netting on walls and ceiling.”

Down the road, builder Doug Saunders bought Bric's Club, gave it a face-lift, renamed it Alfredo's, and began “importing some first-rate acts” — including, in March, Willie Nelson.

Chapter Four

Famous & Infamous

“In the past 70 years, many people have called Castle Hills home.”

Dee Howard, aviator

Thirty-eight patents and a king's airplane

Dee Howard built a full-scale mockup of the Learjet for Bill Lear, and his firm customized aircraft for His Excellency the King of Saudi Arabia and for Chiang Kai-Shek, former President of the Republic of China. At one time, the exhibit notes, Dee Howard was the second-largest employer in San Antonio.

Bill White · raised on E. Castle Lane → Harvard, U.S. Deputy Energy Secretary under President Clinton, and Mayor of Houston
Robert Avalon · Bill White's brother, a pianist and composer collected by Rice University
Orville Carr · ASID Fellow and “the” designer for San Antonio; chosen to design the new airport terminal in 1961
Debora Daniels · TV anchor on KMOL (WOAI) and KSAT, on air from the early '80s until 2004

Sons, daughters & quadruplets

Four girls and a Tonight Show couch

Born in October 1974, the Hansen Quadruplets — Allison, Brooke, Claire, and Darcy — were “San Antonio's babies,” followed by the TV stations and newspapers for every birthday and milestone, and joined at home by older sisters Wendy and Holly. In August 1990 they guest-starred on The Tonight Show with Johnny Carson, and later attended Baylor University.

Also among the “famous and infamous”: “Big John” McClintock, a friend of John Wayne who owned a steak house on Austin Highway and ran a short-lived night club called the Spook House; radio owner Jack Roth; and boxer Jessie James Leija.

Chapter Five

Vice, Crime & the World Series of Poker

The wide-open years — and the West Avenue card games that made history.

6701 West Avenue

The casino that moved to Reno

“Tom Moore's home at 6701 West Avenue was, for many years, an illegal gambling casino that was ignored by law enforcement,” the exhibit recounts — “allegedly because the upper ranks and other important local officials were invited to play and never lost money.”

Eventually the games were raided. Moore abandoned his local efforts and bought a casino in Reno, where he invited the best Texas poker players from his Castle Hills home “to a tournament that became the World Series of Poker.” He would later be called one of the WSOP's “Founding Fathers.”

A separate feature follows 85-year-old Castle Hills resident Gerald Silber, who cashed in a record-field WSOP Seniors Championship and first entered the WSOP Main Event in 2001 as a 70th-birthday gift — back, he said, “when tournament fields were more personable.”

Bombings, bookies & an unsolved party

The cases that made the front page

The boards gather the city's darker headlines: the long bookmaking saga of Jack P. Hanratty; the 1972 pre-dawn bombing of the King Arthur's Court night club, which the FBI tied to a possible extortion plot and to the unsolved killing of club figure Ruby Limon; and the 1961 shotgun death of Mrs. Sabina Dibble at 122 Dogwood, after which a jury found her husband insane.

And the one that was never solved: on New Year's Eve, 1973, aircraft executive Luther Dale Graham, 38, was bludgeoned with a pipe wrench at a party at 111 Briarcliff. “We are never going to close this case,” vowed investigating Sergeant Joe Rosales.

Chapter Six

The Helpers

“Look for the helpers. You will always find people who are helping.”

A city of volunteers

Starting with nothing but a strong desire

The exhibit borrows Mister Rogers' line — “Look for the helpers” — and turns it on the city itself. The first fire department was built by a group of men who “started out with nothing but a strong desire,” raising money with barbecues until they could buy a truck. Every citizen, Yeckel wrote, was involved.

The Castle Hills Woman's Club, founded in 1952, worked to complete the civic center; the Garden Club, the Historical Society, the Homeowners' Association, and the Scouts followed. “We are thankful,” the board reads, “for all the helpers that have come before us and all those that continue to help.”

Seventy-five years on

A safe, friendly, and fun place

From father-and-son police officers in 1955 to a $10,000 community Christmas tree raised by the Public Works crew, the exhibit traces the everyday institutions that kept the city running: National Night Out gatherings, garden-club tree plantings, and the annual Fiesta Castle Hills.

“Over the past 70 years,” the board concludes, “the City of Castle Hills has always had helpers.” In 2026 it celebrates seventy-five.

What the research could not confirm

Honesty is part of the record. These remain open — most would need offline archives (the Bexar County Clerk's incorporation filing, Yeckel's Birth of a City, 1950s city directories, or the council-minutes archive) to settle:

  • The exact incorporation date. Only the year 1951 is documented in public records.
  • The reason for incorporating. The common belief — resisting annexation by San Antonio — has no primary or scholarly source online.
  • The name's origin. The Slimp-family hilltop-home account appears only in the city's own narrative; no deed or directory was found to corroborate it.
  • The full list of mayors. Only the first (Leonard Yeckel) and the present mayor are documented online.
  • The deep past. The 1920s settlement story, Olmos Creek, Indigenous occupation, and early utilities produced no verifiable online record.
